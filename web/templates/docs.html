How this app sources financial data

Every number in the Financials tab traces back to a specific filing — either an XBRL document filed with NSE, a spreadsheet export, or a manually uploaded workbook. When more than one source has reported the same period, this app keeps every observation on file but reconciles a single canonical figure per source's trust ranking, so nothing is silently averaged or overwritten.

The FACT / CALC tags next to each row on the Financials, Overview, and Valuation Model tabs mark whether that figure was reported as-is by the source, or computed here from other reported figures (a ratio, a CAGR, a fallback formula).

{% elif active_section == 'xbrl' %}

About XBRL

XBRL (eXtensible Business Reporting Language) is a structured, tagged format for reporting business and financial data electronically. Instead of a number sitting in an arbitrary spreadsheet cell, XBRL wraps it in a standardized tag drawn from a published taxonomy — a schema defining every reportable item and how it relates to the others for a given regulatory filing. The tagged data itself lives in a separate instance document, which also carries the unit, reporting period, and scale (e.g. crores, lakhs) for each figure. Because every filer uses the same tags, XBRL data can be read and compared programmatically without re-keying it by hand.

NSE requires listed companies to file XBRL for most SEBI (LODR) Regulations compliance disclosures — a separate taxonomy exists per regulation, for example:

  • Regulation 33 — Financial Results (separate taxonomies for Ind-AS companies, banks, NBFCs, and insurers)
  • Regulation 31 — Shareholding Pattern
  • Regulation 27(2) — Corporate Governance
  • Regulation 23(9) — Related Party Transactions
  • Regulation 44 — Voting Results

This app's NSE integration reads the Regulation 33 — Financial Results taxonomy specifically (the quarterly and annual results filing) — both the Ind-AS/general and the banking variants, across the taxonomy-version changes NSE has made over time (including its newer "Integrated Filing" framework). Only what a filing actually reports is used: nothing here is estimated or inferred to fill a gap in the source XBRL.

Source: NSE India, XBRL Filing Information

{% elif active_section == 'research' %}

How Research Works

Asking a research question (Ask AI, or a full Investigation) doesn't just generate one plausible-sounding answer from whatever evidence happens to come up first. It runs through a fixed sequence of stages, so the first plausible narrative isn't automatically the final one:

  1. Hypotheses — the question is broken into competing explanations worth checking, not committed to a single storyline up front.
  2. Plan — for each hypothesis, what evidence would actually confirm or rule it out.
  3. Evidence gathering — retrieval against this company's filings, financials, and prior research.
  4. Evaluation — each hypothesis is scored against what the evidence actually shows.
  5. Sufficiency check — if the evidence gathered so far isn't enough to evaluate confidently, the loop goes back to gather more — but only up to a limit (a fixed number of iterations, wall-clock time, and cost). A hard question can't loop forever or run up unbounded cost; it eventually returns its best answer with the evidence it has, rather than never finishing.
  6. Synthesis — the strongest-evidenced hypothesis becomes the answer, shown as the Verdict at the top of the investigation, with every hypothesis considered listed underneath it.

What you see as "Hypotheses evaluated" and the top Verdict on an Investigation page is this pipeline's actual output, not a summary written after the fact.

{% elif active_section == 'point_in_time' %}

Point-in-Time Investigations

An investigation asking what could genuinely have been concluded as of some past date needs to make sure later information doesn't quietly leak into the answer — hindsight is easy to introduce by accident once a model has seen data that didn't exist yet at the date in question.

When an investigation carries an evidence as of date, every piece of evidence is filtered against that date before it ever reaches the reasoning step — a deterministic cutoff applied to the data itself, not just an instruction asking the model to please ignore later information.

Getting this right means treating several dates on a single piece of evidence as genuinely different things, not one "date" field:

  • the period being reported on (e.g. Q3 FY2025)
  • when it was actually filed or published
  • when this app ingested it
  • when it was later revised or restated, if ever

A figure can be revised months after its original filing — a point-in-time investigation as of a date before that revision uses the original figure, because that's genuinely all that was knowable then.

{% elif active_section == 'model_routing' %}

Choosing an AI Model

Not every question needs the same amount of reasoning. Before any question reaches a model, this app estimates how hard it actually is and routes it to one of three tiers:

  • Quick — a straightforward factual lookup ("what was revenue in FY2024", "current price") — fast and cheap, because no cross-referencing or judgment is needed.
  • Standard — questions that need some synthesis across a moderate amount of evidence, without demanding the deepest reasoning available.
  • Deep — comparisons, trend/outlook questions, "why" questions, or anything involving judgment (risk, sustainability, red flags, discrepancies) — routed to the strongest available model regardless of cost, because getting these right matters more than saving a few cents.

That routing decision is made by a fixed, keyword- and evidence-based rule, not by asking a model to judge its own question's difficulty first — a classifier round-trip like that would cost more than the tokens it's trying to save. The practical effect: a simple factual question typically comes back faster, while a genuinely hard question gets the app's best reasoning, not whatever model happened to be cheapest.

{% elif active_section == 'macro_data' %}

Macro & Economic Data

A company doesn't operate in a vacuum — interest rates, rainfall, and the health of an entire lending sector can all shape how a business performs, even though none of them show up on its own balance sheet. Alongside company-level filings, this app also tracks a handful of these wider economic indicators, each pulled from the official body that actually publishes it. Here's what each one covers and why it's useful to have on hand.

India

RBI (Reserve Bank of India) is India's central bank — the body that sets interest rates, oversees the banking system, and publishes the country's core economic health check-up. This app draws on RBI's own "50 Macroeconomic Indicators" release, which brings together dozens of series — from money supply to banking-system growth — in one place, at whatever cadence RBI itself publishes them (weekly, monthly, or quarterly, depending on the indicator). It's the most direct way to see the broader economic backdrop a company is operating against.

IITM (Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology) publishes India's long-term rainfall records, going back well over a century in some series. That might sound unrelated to a balance sheet, but rainfall drives agricultural income across large parts of the country, which in turn ripples into consumer spending, rural credit demand, and the fortunes of any business whose customers depend on a good monsoon — a genuinely useful macro signal, just one most financial tools skip.

MFIN (Microfinance Institutions Network) is the industry association for India's microfinance lenders — not a government body, but the closest thing to an official voice for that specific corner of the lending sector. Where relevant, this app treats MFIN as a recognized data source for sector-wide microfinance figures, useful context for any company whose business touches small, unsecured rural or semi-urban lending.

United States

FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) is a free, public database maintained by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis — effectively the US equivalent of RBI's own publications, covering things like the Federal Funds Rate (the US central bank's own benchmark interest rate) and 10-Year Treasury yields, alongside hundreds of other series most professional research relies on. Unlike the India sources above, FRED data is fetched live from its own public feed rather than a periodically-updated file, so it stays current with whatever FRED itself has published most recently.

These figures live alongside company financials rather than replacing them — they add context (what was the economic weather like at the time), not a substitute for a company's own reported numbers.
